Who do we trust when advice sounds the same?

If two sources give you exactly the same advice, would you respond differently if one were an AI and the other a human?

For my MSc dissertation, I explored whether changing the stated source of a health recommendation could influence someone's intention to change their routine—and whether existing trust and app use made a difference.

The detail
Context

My MSc Behavioural Science dissertation at the University of Glasgow examined responses to health and lifestyle recommendations labelled as AI-generated or human-generated. The study focused on adults who used health apps.

My contribution

I developed the research question, designed the online experiment, prepared the survey in Qualtrics and analysed responses using regression. I also examined trust and prior app use as possible influences on the relationship between source attribution and behavioural intentions.

Decisions

I kept the recommendation consistent and varied its stated source. This let me examine the effect of the label without also changing the advice people received.

Rather than assume everyone would respond in the same way, I examined whether existing trust changed the effect of source attribution.

What it produced
  • Responses from 119 eligible participants.
  • A seven-item trust measure with high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = .933).
  • No statistically significant source-label effect or interaction between source label and trust.
  • A strong association between baseline and subsequent responses (p < .001).

The findings did not support my original predictions. They also did not establish that source labels never matter; that is a broader claim than this study could make.

What I took away

I expected the source label to matter more than the results supported. The project taught me to separate a plausible explanation from an observed effect—and to treat a result that challenges my hypothesis as something worth understanding, not explaining away.

Understanding elite performers

A product can have an interesting application in theory and still have no obvious place in someone's actual life.

At Neurostellar, I explored what neurotechnology might mean for elite performers, including chess audiences. The work connected customer discovery with a practical question: what would a useful, relevant performance report look like for these people?

The detail
Context

Neurostellar was exploring applications for its technology in specialised performance environments, with their own routines, expectations and definitions of useful information.

My contribution

I worked across customer discovery, validation and insight synthesis, helping translate what we learned into tailored reports and stakeholder presentations.

I also designed customised report layouts in Figma, structured data layers in Google Sheets and helped automate report generation using Python.

Decisions

I approached the audience before assuming what the product should mean to them. I wanted to understand the context surrounding performance, not simply transfer an existing consumer proposition into a specialist environment.

For the reports, I worked backwards from the person receiving the information. The layout and underlying data structure needed to work together, so tailored outputs wouldn't depend entirely on manual assembly.

What it produced
  • 20+ interviews.
  • Four validation sessions.
  • Ten tailored insight reports.
  • Three stakeholder presentations.
  • An audience-specific reporting approach connecting discovery, Figma layouts, structured data and Python-assisted generation.
What I took away

Personalisation starts before the report is designed. It starts with understanding what information matters to someone—and being willing to let that change the output.

Procrastination and habit formation

Why do people put off things they already know they want to do? I explored that gap through neuroscience and behavioural economics, looking beyond the idea that people simply need more motivation.

The detail
Context

An MSc project examining procrastination and habit formation across behavioural and neuroscientific perspectives.

My contribution

I synthesised research on delayed action, the gap between intention and behaviour, and the processes involved in forming habits. I connected these explanations with potential behaviour-change interventions.

Decisions

Rather than treating procrastination as one uniform problem, I examined the conditions that make an action difficult to initiate or repeat—including effort, immediate rewards, context and existing habits.

What it produced

A cross-disciplinary research synthesis connecting procrastination mechanisms, habit formation and potential intervention strategies.

What I took away

Understanding why someone doesn't act is a different problem from telling them what they should do. The conditions surrounding the action deserve attention too.

Making onboarding work better

A wearable can collect all the right signals and still struggle to become part of someone's routine.

At Neurostellar, I worked across onboarding, customer support and product feedback. I became interested in what happened after someone received the device: where they got stuck, what they misunderstood and what we could change to make getting started less of a project in itself.

The detail
Context

An early-stage consumer neurotechnology startup onboarding users across multiple field cohorts. The product involved a wearable and a connected digital experience, so getting started required more than handing someone the hardware.

My contribution

I worked across user onboarding, support processes and feedback loops. I connected recurring user difficulties with improvements to the experience and the internal workflows supporting it.

Decisions

I focused on recurring friction rather than treating every support query as an isolated incident.

Clearer guidance and more repeatable processes could help users get started with less intervention from the team. Support became a source of information about the experience, not just a queue of problems to close.

What it produced
  • Onboarded 300+ wearable users across five field cohorts.
  • Reduced first-week support queries by 90%.
  • Improvements to onboarding guidance, support processes and the feedback loop between users and the team.
What I took away

Adoption doesn't finish when someone receives a device. It is built through the interactions that follow. If the experience keeps asking people to work around it, eventually they stop trying.

A product designed for one context doesn't automatically make sense in another.

Context

An EV-tech business was exploring a potential neurotechnology application, with tailored reporting forming part of the proposed service.

My contribution

I supported opportunity discovery and stakeholder coordination, designed customised report layouts in Figma, organised data layers in Google Sheets and helped automate report generation using Python.

Decisions

I connected the prospective use case with the experience of receiving the output. What would the business need to communicate? What information would make the report useful? How could tailored outputs be produced without rebuilding everything manually?

From prototype to beta production

A prototype is a useful way to test an idea. It is also a very good way to discover how many things you haven't thought about yet.

At Neurostellar, I worked across wearable product development, moving between iterations, design feedback, vendors and manufacturing coordination.

The detail
Context

Wearable hardware development involving industrial design, technical requirements, suppliers and manufacturing.

My contribution

I worked through six independent prototype iterations and coordinated three further versions with designers, vendors and manufacturers. I also coordinated supply-chain and logistics activities for a 50-unit beta production run.

Decisions

The work meant balancing what we wanted the product to become with what could actually be developed and produced. Each iteration was an opportunity to identify practical issues and incorporate feedback.

As the work moved towards production, the focus widened from the object itself to the components, people and dependencies needed to make it.

What it produced
  • Six independent prototype iterations.
  • Three further collaborative versions.

What I took away

A change to one component rarely stays with that component. It can affect the supplier, the next version, the timeline and what the team can realistically promise.

The Invisible Layer

I worked in a startup, which meant a problem could become my problem long before it belonged in my job description.

A delayed component could change a production plan. That could affect inventory, cost and a delivery promise someone had already made. Everyone saw their own part. The connections between those parts were harder to see.

That's the observation behind The Invisible Layer: an independent exploration of what a shared operational picture could look like.

The detail
Context

The idea grew out of my work at Neurostellar and conversations with operators and startups in this space. Building a connected hardware, software and service product made the dependencies particularly visible.

Operational knowledge often lived across conversations, spreadsheets and the heads of the people coordinating everything. Tools captured individual functions, but the connections between decisions were harder to maintain.

My contribution

I mapped the operational relationships I had observed and developed a proposed Ops OS: a shared layer connecting decisions, commitments, blockers and customer promises.

The project brings together an operational map, a product concept and a build plan for testing a small version.

Decisions

I didn't want to propose another task manager or replace every tool a company already uses. I focused on the missing connections between them.

In the proposed workflow, someone provides an update in plain language. The system suggests the relevant records and dependencies, then asks a person to confirm changes before updating the shared picture.

Automation could help surface consequences and prepare actions. It shouldn't quietly decide who is accountable, move money or change a commitment.

What it produced

An interactive operational map, a proposed Ops OS concept and a practical build plan exploring what to capture first, how information could connect and where automation might help.

This is a conceptual proposal, not a deployed product. The next step is to test whether a small team would use this shared layer in everyday work, what information it would need and who would keep it accurate.

What I took away

A company can have plenty of tools and still struggle to understand what's happening. The difficult part isn't always recording a decision. It's seeing what that decision changes elsewhere.

Taking a product to the exhibition floor

A technology can be impressive in a product meeting and completely confusing to someone encountering it for the first time.

I worked on bringing Neurostellar to GITEX Global in Dubai and CES in Las Vegas, where product demonstrations, commercial conversations and last-minute troubleshooting all met.

The detail
Context

Neurostellar showcased its consumer neurotechnology at GITEX Global 2024 and CES 2025, including Eureka Park.

My contribution

I worked across exhibition preparation, booth and experience design, marketing materials, product demonstrations and commercial conversations.

For GITEX, I coordinated exhibition delivery and represented Neurostellar within a startup portfolio associated with the Tamil Nadu IT Ministry. At CES, I contributed to the preparation and delivery of the company's presence at Eureka Park.

Decisions

The challenge was to make a technical product understandable in a busy exhibition environment. The booth, materials, explanation and demonstration needed to work together.

I also focused on the practical coordination behind those interactions—and being ready when the floor didn't go according to plan.

What it produced

The wider team's commercial activity generated:

  • 250 leads at GITEX.
  • 480+ leads at CES.
  • Five partnership opportunities and three on-floor conversions across the two events.
What I took away

A product experience starts before someone becomes a user. The explanation, environment and conversation all shape whether someone understands what you're building and why it might matter.
